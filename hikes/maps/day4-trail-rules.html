On the Trail

GLACIER NATIONAL PARK
GRIZZLY COUNTRY

Pack This

Ticked boxes we're organizing as a group.

  • Bear spray — on a hip or chest strap, not buried in the pack
  • 2–3 L water each, plus a filter or tablets
  • Lunch and more snacks than you think you need
  • A gallon zip as the group trash bag
  • TP and a second zip to carry it back out
  • Hand sanitizer
  • Rain shell and a warm layer — Logan Pass is cold and windy even in August
  • Sun — hat, sunscreen, sunglasses
  • Offline map downloaded before you lose service
  • Headlamp on the long days
  • First aid and blister tape

!Bears: don't surprise one

Almost every bad encounter starts with a bear that had no idea you were coming. Your job is to never be a surprise.

  • Make noise — constantly. Talk, and keep talking. Call out at blind corners, in thick brush, near berry patches, and anywhere running water is covering the sound of you.
  • Your voice beats any gadget. Bear bells are too quiet and sound nothing like a person. Use words, out loud, every minute or so.
  • Hike as a group of three or more, and stay together. Larger groups are charged far less often. Nobody races ahead, nobody trails behind, nobody puts in headphones.
  • Dawn, dusk and poor visibility are the riskiest hours — be louder then.
  • Trails close for bear activity, and a closure is not a suggestion.
  • Stay 100 yards from any bear. That is the rule in the park, and no photo is worth closing the gap.

2If you actually see one

  • Stop. Do not run. Running triggers a chase, and a grizzly is faster than you over any distance.
  • Group up and stand tall together. Pick up small children.
  • Talk to it in a low, level voice so it reads you as human.
  • Back away slowly, sideways, watching it. Leave it an exit — never corner a bear or come between a sow and cubs.
  • Keep your pack on. It is body armour, and dropping it teaches a bear that people mean food.
If it charges
  • Most charges are bluffs. Stand your ground and get the spray out of its holster.
  • Spray at roughly 30–60 feet: aim slightly down and lay a cloud between you and the bear. Keep spraying as it comes through.
  • Grizzly that makes contact — play dead. Face down, legs spread, hands laced behind your neck, pack on. Stay down until you are certain it is gone.
  • Black bear, or any bear stalking you — fight back. Hard, at the face, with anything you have.

FFood & water

  • Calorie-dense, no cooking: nuts, jerky, hard cheese, tortillas with nut butter, dried fruit, bars.
  • Salt matters as much as sugar on a six-to-eight hour day. Bring electrolytes, not just water.
  • 2–3 L each. Refill only through a filter or tablets — giardia is in these streams however clean the water looks.
  • Eat where you can see around you — in the open, not in thick brush.
  • Food stays in the pack; the pack stays with you. Not left on a rock for a photo, not at the shore for a swim.

TTrash: everything gets carried out

  • Food waste is trash too — apple cores, orange peel, pistachio shells, crusts, crumbs from the bottom of the bag.
  • "It's natural, it'll rot" doesn't work here. It breaks down slowly, and it teaches animals to look for food where people walk.
  • One person carries the group trash zip.
  • Micro-trash counts: wrapper tear-offs, tape, twist ties, fruit stickers.
  • Nothing goes down a pit toilet except what a body makes.

BBathroom, done properly

  • Go at the trailhead. Avalanche, Logan Pass and Many Glacier all have toilets.
  • Otherwise: 200 feet — about 70 adult paces — from water, trail and camp.
  • Dig a cathole 6–8 inches deep in dark soil, then fill it in and scatter leaf litter over it.
  • Toilet paper and hygiene products pack out, in a doubled opaque zip — TP doesn't break down quickly, and used products carry scent.
  • Pee on rock or gravel — animals dig up salty soil and wreck the plants around it.
  • Take your bear spray with you and tell someone where you went. Stepping off the trail alone and quiet is precisely the situation the noise rule exists for.
